\section{Practical Motivation and the Edge Voice Problem}
Personalized speech synthesis offers substantial utility across assistive and educational tools, but conventional architectures face practical limits in privacy-sensitive or offline settings. Consider several concrete scenarios: a rural schoolteacher in Odisha who requires an interactive reading assistant that speaks in her own voice in Odia, where student voice recordings cannot leave the local tablet; a visually impaired individual in Tamil Nadu using an offline navigation tool configured with a family member's voice; or an individual undergoing speech rehabilitation in a medical ward who needs a personalized communication aid without internet access. 

In these settings, remote cloud processing is impractical due to privacy requirements, connectivity gaps, or latency. However, existing production systems do not support zero-shot multilingual voice cloning that runs fully offline on resource-constrained mobile hardware for Indian languages. This project investigates how to bridge that gap.

\subsection{Voice Identity as a Biometric Signal}
A human speech signal jointly conveys linguistic content, speaker identity, prosody, and acoustic environment characteristics within a few seconds of audio. The problem of zero-shot voice cloning requires synthesizing arbitrary new text in the voice of an unseen speaker using only a brief reference recording (typically three to five seconds), without modifying model parameters or running gradient updates during inference.

While recent generative speech models achieve high perceptual naturalness, existing deployments operate almost exclusively on cloud servers equipped with high-end GPUs. This paradigm introduces three clear issues:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Biometric Privacy Exposure}: Transmitting unencrypted or raw speech samples to external servers exposes biometric identifiers to data interception, unauthorized secondary use, and spoofing risks.
    \item \textbf{Limited Language Coverage}: Over one billion individuals speak Dravidian and Indo-Aryan languages. Most open-source and commercial edge voice cloning models support only English or Mandarin.
    \item \textbf{Network Dependency}: In rural schools, transit corridors, and disaster response areas across the Indian subcontinent, internet connectivity is often intermittent or unavailable, rendering cloud-dependent assistants unusable.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evolution of Speech Synthesis Paradigms}
Automated text-to-speech (\gls{tts}) architectures have developed across three major generations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Formant synthesis} modeled the human vocal tract through systems of acoustic resonators. Although computationally lightweight, the generated audio sounded unnatural and metallic.
    \item \textit{Concatenative synthesis} assembled waveforms from multi-hour databases of pre-recorded speech fragments. This approach yielded high segmental naturalness, but required hundreds of megabytes of storage per voice, could not modify prosody dynamically, and required recording an entire dataset to support a new speaker.
    \item \textit{Statistical parametric speech synthesis} (SPSS) modeled acoustic features using Hidden Markov Models. While SPSS reduced storage requirements and permitted parametric voice transformations, over-smoothing often produced muffled output.
\end{enumerate}

The introduction of deep neural networks led to end-to-end architectures such as Tacotron~2, FastSpeech, and \gls{vits}~\cite{kim2021vits}, alongside neural vocoders such as WaveNet and \gls{hifigan}~\cite{kong2020hifigan}, which achieved human-like Mean Opinion Scores (\gls{mos}). Nevertheless, traditional neural pipelines remain ill-suited for mobile deployment: they require minutes of clean audio for speaker adaptation, and their multi-stage architectures generally demand high-power GPUs.

Modern zero-shot in-context voice cloning models address this limitation by conditioning generation directly on short reference clips without gradient updates. This investigation adapts and optimizes that paradigm for on-device deployment across Indian languages.

\section{The Zero-Shot In-Context Voice Cloning Paradigm}
Formally, zero-shot voice cloning denotes the capacity of an acoustic model to synthesize arbitrary target text in the vocal identity, pitch range, and cadence of an unseen speaker, conditioned solely on a brief reference audio prompt (three to five seconds), without parameter fine-tuning.

Let $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T_{\text{ref}}}$ denote the intermediate acoustic representation (such as a 100-channel log-mel spectrogram) extracted from a reference recording of duration $T_{\text{ref}}$, and let $\mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}} = (w_1^{\text{ref}}, w_2^{\text{ref}}, \dots, w_{N_{\text{ref}}}^{\text{ref}})$ denote its linguistic transcript. Given a novel target text sequence $\mathbf{w}_{\text{tgt}} = (w_1^{\text{tgt}}, w_2^{\text{tgt}}, \dots, w_{N_{\text{tgt}}}^{\text{tgt}})$, zero-shot speech synthesis models the conditional distribution:
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \mid \mathbf{w}_{\text{tgt}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}}; \theta)
    \label{eq:zero_shot_conditional}
\end{equation}
such that the synthesized target mel-spectrogram $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T_{\text{tgt}}}$ matches the text sequence $\mathbf{w}_{\text{tgt}}$ while replicating the acoustic identity of $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}$.

Early zero-shot systems relied on speaker encoder networks trained with contrastive objectives (such as the generalized end-to-end loss). These encoders projected variable-length audio into fixed-dimensional speaker embeddings (d-vectors or x-vectors) $\mathbf{e}_{\text{spk}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, typically with $D \in [256, 512]$. The acoustic model was conditioned on $\mathbf{e}_{\text{spk}}$ via feature concatenation or adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) layers.

However, global embedding vectors introduce an information bottleneck: a single static vector struggles to preserve fine-grained temporal cues, including prosodic variations, co-articulation patterns, and room reverberation characteristics.

To address this limitation, recent models such as Voicebox~\cite{le2023voicebox}, \gls{e2tts}~\cite{e2tts2024}, and F5-TTS~\cite{chen2024f5tts} frame zero-shot speech synthesis as an audio inpainting problem. Instead of compressing the reference signal into a single vector, continuous acoustic frames $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}$ are concatenated directly with the target generation trajectory along the time axis. The model uses self-attention and cross-attention mechanisms to learn dependencies between the reference acoustics and the target phonetic sequence, harmonizing speaker timbre across the transition boundary.

\section[Rationale for On-Device Edge Execution]{Rationale for On-Device Edge Execution: Privacy, Connectivity, and Operational Costs}
Although cloud-based voice cloning systems achieve strong fidelity on server GPUs, relying on remote infrastructure introduces three practical concerns:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Biometric Data Privacy}: A voice recording is an immutable biometric identifier. Cloud services require uploading raw speech to external servers, creating risks of interception, data retention, and unauthorized voice cloning. Performing inference entirely on the edge device guarantees that user audio never leaves local memory.
    
    \item \textbf{Reliable Offline Operation}: In many regions of the Indian subcontinent, internet access remains variable or absent. In rural education centers, transit corridors, and disaster recovery areas, continuous network access cannot be assumed. Edge execution ensures deterministic, uninterrupted operation in airplane mode.
    
    \item \textbf{Elimination of Cloud Infrastructure Costs}: Cloud synthesis APIs incur recurring per-minute and per-character fees driven by GPU server maintenance. Running models directly on consumer devices eliminates cloud infrastructure costs entirely, making personalized speech accessible at scale.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Tri-Factor Challenge in On-Device Edge Deployment}
Deploying continuous flow-matching models on consumer mobile devices exposes a multi-constraint optimization problem, which we designate as the Tri-Factor Challenge:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Native Indic and Dravidian Phonological Support}: Over 1.4 billion people speak Dravidian and Indo-Aryan languages. These languages feature distinct phonetic inventories, agglutinative morphology, syllabic akshara scripts, consonant conjuncts (\textit{samyuktaksharas}), and dependent vowel signs (\textit{matras}). Western and Chinese zero-shot models (e.g., Kokoro-82M, standard F5-TTS, or ZipVoice-Distill) lack Indic character vocabularies and phonetic coverage, making them incapable of synthesizing Indian languages.
    
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot In-Context Generalization}: The system must adapt to an unseen speaker from a brief, consumer-grade smartphone recording without gradient updates. Autoregressive models (e.g., XTTS-v2 at 467M parameters~\cite{casanova2024xtts}) suffer from $O(T)$ sequential latency, quadratic attention memory, and hallucination loops. Non-autoregressive flow-matching models generate frames in parallel, but require low-step numerical solvers to remain feasible on mobile hardware.
    
    \item \textbf{Strict Physical Edge Memory and Compute Budgets}: The pipeline must run entirely on-device without cloud connectivity. Commodity edge devices have limited RAM, bounded memory bandwidth, thermal constraints, and heterogeneous CPU clusters without dedicated NPUs.
\end{enumerate}

Existing open-source and commercial models typically address at most two of these requirements: models that run on-device are limited to English and Chinese; models that support Indian languages rely on remote server clusters; and lightweight on-device engines are restricted to single-speaker statistical synthesis. This investigation targets the intersection of all three criteria.

\section{Embedded Hardware Constraints and Operating System Resource Envelopes}
To design an edge architecture capable of practical mass deployment, our engineering target is anchored against budget commodity hardware rather than flagship silicon. In this project, our primary reference hardware is the \textbf{Lenovo Tab K10}, with the following specifications:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System-on-Chip (\gls{soc})}: MediaTek Helio P22T (MT8768T), built on a 12\,nm FinFET process.
    \item \textbf{Processor Microarchitecture}: An octa-core ARM Cortex-A53 cluster in a big.LITTLE configuration (4 cores up to 2.3\,GHz and 4 efficiency cores up to 1.8\,GHz). The Cortex-A53 implements an in-order execution pipeline with dual-issue capabilities, lacking modern out-of-order execution optimizations.
    \item \textbf{Vector Acceleration}: Standard ARMv8-A \gls{neon} 128-bit Single Instruction Multiple Data (\gls{simd}) execution units.
    \item \textbf{Neural Processing Hardware}: Zero dedicated NPU or AI accelerator. All deep learning inference operations (matrix multiplications, convolutional projections, and Fourier transformations) execute on the CPU cluster.
    \item \textbf{Physical System Memory}: 3\,GB or 4\,GB LPDDR4X \gls{ram} over a 32-bit dual-channel memory bus with a theoretical peak bandwidth capped at approximately 14.9\,GB/s.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Linux Kernel LowMemoryKiller (LMK) Paradigm}
Memory management in Android is governed by the Linux kernel out-of-memory (\gls{lmk}) driver. Unlike desktop operating systems (Linux, Windows, macOS) that use disk paging space to absorb memory spikes, Android disables swap-to-flash storage to protect mobile eMMC and UFS storage chips from write-endurance degradation.

Android instead assigns \texttt{oom\_adj\_score} values to processes. Foreground applications receive low scores but must remain within an absolute physical memory ceiling. On a device with 4\,GB of physical RAM, the operating system kernel, system services (SurfaceFlinger, SystemUI), background daemons, and radio baseband stacks consume between 2.2\,GB and 2.5\,GB of resident RAM. Consequently, the user-space foreground process has an available physical \gls{rss} budget strictly capped at:
\begin{equation}
    \text{RSS}_{\text{max}} \approx 1,500\,\text{MB} \quad \text{to} \quad 1,800\,\text{MB}
\end{equation}
If an executing model allocates tensor activation buffers, dynamic memory arenas, or uncompressed weights that exceed this ceiling, the Linux kernel issues an immediate, uncatchable \texttt{SIGKILL} signal.

Standard FP32 models illustrate this risk: the unquantized IndicF5 PyTorch model requires 1.35\,GB for model weights alone, and its dynamic activation buffers require an additional 1.2\,GB during generation. This pushes process memory beyond 2.5\,GB, triggering an immediate SIGKILL termination on a 4\,GB Android device. Consequently, integer quantization, graph optimization, and pre-allocated memory arenas are structural requirements for on-device deployment.

\section{Evaluation Framework for Edge Speech Generative Systems}
To evaluate on-device speech synthesis, we define an evaluation framework balancing physical hardware constraints, vocal cloning fidelity, and inference throughput:

\subsection[Physical Memory Confinement and Peak RSS]{Physical Memory Confinement and Peak Resident Set Size (\gls{rss})}
The core operational criterion for on-device viability is physical memory confinement. Memory consumption is quantified not by virtual address reservations (\texttt{VmSize}), which reflect mapped address space, but by Peak Resident Set Size (\texttt{VmRSS}), which measures physical RAM allocated to the process by the kernel memory subsystem. Peak \gls{rss} is monitored by polling \texttt{/proc/self/status} at the peak of the diffusion integration loop. For edge stability on 4\,GB platforms, peak resident memory must remain strictly below 1,000\,MB, leaving sufficient headroom for the host application and Android framework services.

\subsection{Storage Footprint and Package Compressibility}
On consumer mobile hardware, application package size directly affects user adoption. Large desktop checkpoints requiring gigabytes of disk space are impractical for distribution. We evaluate models based on their quantized on-disk footprint, targeting $\le 400$\,MB for the combined acoustic model and neural vocoder.

\subsection{Acoustic and Speaker Identity Transfer Fidelity}
Voice cloning fidelity is evaluated along two complementary dimensions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Speaker Similarity and Naturalness}: Measured through Mean Opinion Scores (\gls{mos}) on a 5-point Likert scale, assessing how faithfully the synthesized audio replicates the timbre, pitch contour, and perceived identity of the reference speaker.
    \item \textbf{Acoustic Spectral Distortion}: Measured via Mel-Cepstral Distortion (\gls{mcd}) between ground-truth and synthesized acoustic trajectories:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MCD} = \frac{10\sqrt{2}}{\ln 10} \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^D (c_t[d] - \hat{c}_t[d])^2}
    \end{equation}
    where $c_t[d]$ and $\hat{c}_t[d]$ denote the $d$-th mel-frequency cepstral coefficients of target and synthesized frames.
\end{itemize}

\subsection[Computational Throughput and RTF]{Computational Throughput and Real-Time Factor (\gls{rtf})}
Operational throughput is quantified via the \gls{rtf}, defined as the ratio between the total wall-clock computational latency required to synthesize the audio and the physical duration of the resulting synthesized speech waveform:
\begin{equation}
    \text{RTF} = \frac{T_{\text{synthesis}}}{T_{\text{audio}}} = \frac{\Delta t_{\text{proc}}}{\frac{N_{\text{samples}}}{f_s}}
    \label{eq:rtf_definition}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_{\text{proc}}$ represents execution time in seconds, $N_{\text{samples}}$ denotes synthesized audio samples, and $f_s$ represents the acoustic sampling frequency (24,000\,Hz).

In edge environments lacking discrete GPUs, the immediate goal is to establish stable, deterministic execution latencies on mobile ARM CPUs, providing an empirical baseline for future NPU acceleration.

\subsection{The Fixed Overhead Anomaly in Short Utterances}
In iterative flow-matching systems, empirical latency characterization reveals an effect we term the Fixed Overhead Anomaly. In traditional autoregressive systems, computational latency scales linearly with output sequence length ($O(T)$). However, in non-autoregressive continuous flow matching on edge hardware, total synthesis latency decomposes into fixed initialization overhead and length-dependent numerical solving:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{synthesis}} = T_{\text{init}} + N_{\text{steps}} \cdot T_{\text{forward}}(T_{\text{audio}}) + T_{\text{vocoder}}(T_{\text{audio}})
    \label{eq:latency_decomposition}
\end{equation}
Here, $T_{\text{init}}$ encompasses tokenization, vocabulary lookup, text embedding projections, runtime memory arena configuration, and vocoder setup. On mobile ARM hardware, $T_{\text{init}}$ represents an invariant baseline cost of approximately 1.0 to 1.5 seconds.

Consequently, when synthesizing a short sentence (e.g., a five-word utterance yielding $T_{\text{audio}} \approx 1.2$\,seconds), the invariant overhead $T_{\text{init}}$ dominates the numerator of \eref{eq:rtf_definition}, producing an elevated \gls{rtf} (e.g., $\text{RTF} \in [3.8, 9.3]$). As target audio duration increases to medium ($T_{\text{audio}} \approx 5.5$\,s) and long ($T_{\text{audio}} \approx 15.0$\,s) sequences, $T_{\text{init}}$ amortizes over the larger denominator, allowing the measured \gls{rtf} to decrease toward the model's steady-state forward-pass throughput on physical ARM cores.
