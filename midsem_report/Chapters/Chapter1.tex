\section{The Grand Challenge: Your Voice, On Your Device, In Your Language}
Imagine a rural schoolteacher in Odisha who wants an interactive reading assistant that speaks in her own voice—in Odia—without her voice recordings ever leaving her tablet. Or a visually impaired farmer in Tamil Nadu who needs an offline navigation assistant that responds in Tamil, cloned from a five-second recording of a family member. Or a speech therapy patient rehearsing their vocal identity in a hospital ward with zero internet access. These are not speculative futures; they are immediate, urgent human needs separated from technical reality by a single gap: \textit{there exists no system capable of performing zero-shot multilingual voice cloning entirely on a resource-constrained mobile device, offline, for Indian languages.}

This investigation is a direct attempt to close that gap.

\subsection{The Voice as a Biometric Identity}
The human voice is unlike any other data modality. It simultaneously encodes linguistic content, speaker identity, emotional state, physiological characteristics, and acoustic room fingerprint—all within a signal spanning a few seconds. The scientific problem of \textit{voice cloning} asks: given a brief reference recording, can a machine synthesize arbitrary new speech that is perceptually indistinguishable from the original speaker? Recent frontier neural architectures have answered this affirmatively—but only in the cloud, only in English or Mandarin, and only when servers equipped with enterprise GPUs are available.

The consequences of this status quo are profound:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Biometric Privacy Violation}: Uploading raw voice recordings to cloud servers exposes immutable biometric identifiers to interception, unauthorized model training, and deepfake exploitation.
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of 1.4 Billion Speakers}: Over one billion people communicate in Dravidian and Indo-Aryan languages. No existing zero-shot edge voice cloning system supports even a single Indian language.
    \item \textbf{Connectivity Dependence}: In rural education centers, disaster zones, and bandwidth-constrained transit corridors across the Indian subcontinent, server-dependent systems simply fail to function.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Evolutionary Arc of Speech Synthesis}
The field of automated speech synthesis—commonly designated as \gls{tts}—has undergone profound paradigm shifts over the preceding three decades. Early \textit{formant synthesis} modeled the human vocal tract via systems of differential equations: computationally lightweight and explainable, but producing metallic, robotic output. \textit{Concatenative synthesis} then redefined the state-of-the-art by stitching acoustic fragments from multi-hour speaker databases—achieving unprecedented local naturalness at the cost of gigabytes of storage and total rigidity. Synthesizing a new speaker required re-recording an entire corpus from scratch.

\textit{Statistical parametric synthesis} (SPSS) via Hidden Markov Models compressed speech into compact probability distributions, enabling mathematical voice morphing. Yet excessive spectral smoothing yielded muffled, lifeless waveforms. The deep learning revolution transformed this landscape: end-to-end neural architectures—Tacotron, Tacotron~2, FastSpeech, and \gls{vits}~\cite{kim2021vits}—paired with neural vocoders such as WaveNet and \gls{hifigan}~\cite{kong2020hifigan} finally achieved human-like Mean Opinion Scores (\gls{mos}).

However, conventional neural \gls{tts} pipelines remain constrained by two fundamental limitations. First, they operate in \textit{closed-speaker settings}, requiring tens of minutes of clean recordings to adapt to an individual speaker. Second, their deployment has historically relied on high-power \glspl{gpu}, making them fundamentally ill-suited for edge and mobile environments.

The modern frontier is \textit{zero-shot in-context voice cloning}: an acoustic generative model that adapts to an entirely unseen speaker from a brief three-to-five second audio clip, with no parameter updates. This is the technical paradigm this investigation targets for the Indian subcontinent.

\section{The Zero-Shot In-Context Voice Cloning Paradigm}
In contemporary human-computer interaction, telecommunications, accessibility software, and embedded virtual assistants, the requirement for \textit{zero-shot neural voice cloning} has emerged as a paramount technical objective. Formally, zero-shot voice cloning denotes the capability of an acoustic generative model to synthesize arbitrary target speech in the precise vocal identity, pitch range, formant characteristics, and acoustic cadence of an unseen human speaker, conditioned solely upon a brief \textit{reference audio prompt} (typically three to five seconds in duration), without modifying model parameters $\theta$ or performing gradient-based test-time fine-tuning.

Mathematically, let $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T_{\text{ref}}}$ denote the intermediate acoustic representation (such as a 100-channel log-mel spectrogram) extracted from a reference speech signal of duration $T_{\text{ref}}$, and let $\mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}} = (w_1^{\text{ref}}, w_2^{\text{ref}}, \dots, w_{N_{\text{ref}}}^{\text{ref}})$ represent its corresponding linguistic transcript. Given a novel target text sequence $\mathbf{w}_{\text{tgt}} = (w_1^{\text{tgt}}, w_2^{\text{tgt}}, \dots, w_{N_{\text{tgt}}}^{\text{tgt}})$, zero-shot speech synthesis seeks to estimate the conditional distribution:
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \mid \mathbf{w}_{\text{tgt}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}, \mathbf{w}_{\text{ref}}; \theta)
    \label{eq:zero_shot_conditional}
\end{equation}
such that the synthesized target mel-spectrogram $\mathbf{x}_{\text{tgt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T_{\text{tgt}}}$ simultaneously maintains verbatim linguistic fidelity to $\mathbf{w}_{\text{tgt}}$ and acoustic identity alignment with $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}$.

Historically, early attempts to realize zero-shot cloning relied upon \textit{speaker encoder networks} trained via generalized contrastive losses (e.g., GE2E loss). These encoders mapped variable-length reference audio clips into fixed-dimensional speaker embedding vectors (d-vectors or x-vectors) $\mathbf{e}_{\text{spk}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ (where typically $D \in [256, 512]$). The acoustic generator was subsequently conditioned on $\mathbf{e}_{\text{spk}}$ through concatenation or adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) layers. 

While theoretically elegant, global embedding vectors suffer from severe information bottlenecks: a single static vector is incapable of capturing fine-grained dynamic acoustic properties, such as subtle prosodic variations, co-articulation shifts, phoneme-specific timbre nuances, and room reverberation profiles.

To overcome the limitations of static speaker embeddings, modern frontier models—most notably Voicebox~\cite{le2023voicebox}, \gls{e2tts}~\cite{e2tts2024}, and F5-TTS~\cite{chen2024f5tts}—reframe zero-shot voice cloning as an \textit{in-context audio inpainting problem}. Rather than compressing the reference signal into a low-dimensional bottleneck, the continuous acoustic frames $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}$ are concatenated directly with the target generation trajectory along the temporal dimension. The neural network leverages cross-attention and bidirectional self-attention mechanisms to learn long-range temporal dependencies directly between the reference acoustics and the target phonetic sequence. Consequently, the generative model learns to fill in the missing acoustic canvas while harmonizing global and local speaker characteristics across the temporal boundary.

\section[The Imperative for Edge Execution]{The Imperative for Edge Execution: Privacy, Data\\Sovereignty, and Offline Autonomy}
While in-context neural voice cloning has attained remarkable fidelity in cloud data centers equipped with multi-GPU clusters, the overwhelming reliance on remote server infrastructure introduces profound ethical, operational, and practical vulnerabilities:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Biometric Data Privacy and Identity Sovereignty}: The human voice is not merely an arbitrary acoustic signal; it constitutes a sensitive, immutable biological biometric identifier. Cloud-based voice cloning services require users to transmit raw recordings of their voiceprints to external server clusters. In transit and at rest, these biometric assets are vulnerable to interception, unauthorized model training, surveillance, and adversarial voice identity theft (deepfake impersonation). Confining the entire generative cloning pipeline strictly to the physical edge device establishes an absolute privacy guarantee: raw audio samples, intermediate representations, and identity parameters never depart the user's personal hardware.
    
    \item \textbf{100\% Offline Autonomy in Low-Connectivity Regions}: Across the Indian subcontinent and developing nations, ubiquitous high-speed internet connectivity remains an elusive assumption. In rural education centers, transit corridors, remote agricultural administrative kiosks, and disaster recovery zones, network connections are frequently intermittent, high-latency, or completely unavailable. A voice cloning system that demands continuous server access fails precisely where assistive and educational technologies are most urgently needed. True edge execution guarantees deterministic, uninterrupted operation in complete airplane mode.
    
    \item \textbf{Democratization and Zero Infrastructure Cost}: Commercial cloud-based voice synthesis APIs levy recurring per-character or per-minute operational fees, driven by the extreme capital expenditure of enterprise graphics cards (such as NVIDIA A100 or H100 clusters). By transferring the entire computational burden onto commodity consumer hardware already owned by the end user, on-device voice cloning completely eliminates cloud server infrastructure costs, democratizing personalized speech synthesis at scale.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Tri-Factor Challenge in On-Device Edge Deployment}
Deploying high-capacity continuous flow-matching models directly onto consumer mobile and embedded devices introduces an extraordinarily complex multi-objective optimization problem. We identify this fundamental engineering tension as the \textbf{Tri-Factor Challenge in Edge Speech Synthesis}, defined by the non-overlapping intersection of three distinct engineering requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Native Indic and Tamil Phonological Support}: Over 1.4 billion people communicate in Dravidian and Indo-Aryan languages. These languages feature unique phonetic inventories, agglutinative morphology, intricate syllabic scripts (\textit{aksharas}), consonant conjuncts (\textit{samyuktaksharas}), and dependent vowel signs (\textit{matras}). Western and Chinese zero-shot models (e.g., Kokoro-82M, standard F5-TTS, or ZipVoice-Distill) are completely devoid of Indic character vocabularies, phonetic lexicons, and grapheme-to-phoneme (\gls{g2p}) rules, rendering them entirely incapable of synthesizing intelligible Indian speech.
    
    \item \textbf{True Zero-Shot In-Context Cloning}: The architecture must deliver instantaneous speaker adaptation from an arbitrary, in-the-wild audio clip recorded on a consumer smartphone microphone. Autoregressive language-model-based speech architectures (e.g., XTTS-v2 at 467M parameters~\cite{casanova2024xtts}) suffer from sequential inference latency ($O(T)$ forward passes), quadratic attention memory scaling, and severe susceptibility to phonetic hallucinations and looping babble. Conversely, non-autoregressive flow-matching architectures generate entire spectrograms in parallel, but traditionally demand intensive iterative numerical solving.
    
    \item \textbf{Strict Edge Execution and Hardware Resource Budgets}: The entire synthesis pipeline must execute strictly offline on physical device hardware without transmitting user voice samples to remote cloud infrastructure. However, commodity edge hardware is characterized by severely constrained random-access memory (\gls{ram}), bounded memory bandwidth, thermal throttling ceilings, and heterogeneous processor architectures.
\end{enumerate}

Virtually all existing commercial and open-source models satisfy at most two of these three pillars simultaneously: models that support zero-shot cloning on edge devices are restricted to English and Chinese; models that support Indian languages are monolithic cloud-only services; and models that run locally on low-end hardware are rigid, single-speaker concatenative or basic statistical engines. This investigation systematically addresses the vacant intersection of all three criteria.

\section{Embedded Hardware Constraints and Operating System Resource Envelopes}
To design an edge architecture capable of practical mass deployment, the engineering design must not assume flagship mobile silicon; rather, it must be anchored against the strict computational and memory envelopes of lower-tier, budget commodity hardware. In this investigation, our primary baseline deployment target is the \textbf{Lenovo Tab K10}, characterized by the following physical specifications:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System-on-Chip (\gls{soc})}: MediaTek Helio P22T (MT8768T), fabricated on a 12\,nm FinFET process.
    \item \textbf{Processor Microarchitecture}: An octa-core ARM Cortex-A53 cluster configured in a big.LITTLE arrangement (4 cores clocked up to 2.3\,GHz and 4 efficiency cores clocked up to 1.8\,GHz). Notably, the Cortex-A53 core implements an in-order execution pipeline with dual-issue capabilities, lacking modern out-of-order execution optimizations.
    \item \textbf{Vector Acceleration}: Standard ARMv8-A \gls{neon} 128-bit Single Instruction Multiple Data (\gls{simd}) execution units.
    \item \textbf{Neural Processing Hardware}: \textbf{Zero dedicated \gls{npu} or AI accelerator}. All deep learning inference operations—including matrix-vector multiplications, convolutional projections, and Fourier transformations—must execute entirely on the CPU cluster.
    \item \textbf{Physical System Memory}: 3\,GB or 4\,GB LPDDR4X \gls{ram} operating over a 32-bit dual-channel memory bus with a theoretical peak bandwidth capped at approximately 14.9\,GB/s.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Linux Kernel LowMemoryKiller (LMK) Paradigm}
In the Android operating system, memory management is fundamentally governed by the Linux kernel's out-of-memory (\gls{lmk}) driver. Unlike desktop operating systems (Windows, Linux, macOS) which transparently utilize disk swap space (paging files) to absorb large memory spikes, Android deliberately disables swap-to-flash storage to protect mobile eMMC and UFS storage chips from accelerated write-endurance degradation. 

Instead, Android implements a strict memory scoring hierarchy using \texttt{oom\_adj\_score} values. Foreground applications operate with a minimal score, but are bounded by an absolute physical memory ceiling. On a device with 4\,GB of physical RAM, the operating system kernel, system services (SurfaceFlinger, SystemUI), background daemons, and radio baseband stacks consume between 2.2\,GB and 2.5\,GB of resident RAM. Consequently, the user-space foreground process possesses an available physical \gls{rss} budget strictly capped at:
\begin{equation}
    \text{RSS}_{\text{max}} \approx 1,500\,\text{MB} \quad \text{to} \quad 1,800\,\text{MB}
\end{equation}
Should an executing machine learning model instantiate tensor activation buffers, dynamic memory arenas, or uncompressed weights that push the process's physical footprint beyond this ceiling, the Linux kernel issues an immediate, non-catchable \texttt{SIGKILL} signal. 

Standard FP32 neural generative models—such as the base IndicF5 PyTorch model requiring 1.35\,GB for weights alone, plus dynamic activation buffers scaling upwards of 1.2\,GB—instantly exceed 2.5\,GB of resident memory, causing catastrophic operating system termination. Hence, model compression, post-training quantization, and static memory arena allocation are not merely efficiency optimizations; they represent fundamental operational prerequisites for physical edge viability.

\section{Evaluation Framework for Edge Speech Generative Systems}
To evaluate on-device speech synthesis, we establish an evaluation framework balancing physical hardware confinement, vocal cloning fidelity, and operational execution throughput:

\subsection[Physical Memory Confinement and Peak RSS]{Physical Memory Confinement and Peak Resident Set Size (\gls{rss})}
The definitive operational criterion for on-device viability is absolute physical memory confinement. Memory consumption is quantified not by virtual address reservations (\texttt{VmSize}), which merely reflect mapped address space, but by \textit{Peak Resident Set Size} (\texttt{VmRSS}), which measures the exact quantity of physical RAM allocated to the process by the kernel's memory management subsystem. Peak \gls{rss} is continuously monitored by interrogating the pseudo-filesystem node \texttt{/proc/self/status} during the apex of the diffusion integration loop. For edge stability on 4\,GB platforms, peak resident memory must remain strictly below 1,000\,MB, leaving sufficient headroom for the host application and Android framework services.

\subsection{Storage Footprint and Package Compressibility}
On consumer mobile hardware, application package size directly governs user adoption and distribution feasibility. Monolithic desktop checkpoints requiring gigabytes of disk space are wholly impractical. We evaluate models based on their quantized on-disk footprint, establishing a strict target threshold of $\le 400$\,MB for the combined acoustic model and neural vocoder.

\subsection{Acoustic and Speaker Identity Transfer Fidelity}
Voice cloning fidelity is evaluated along two complementary dimensions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Speaker Similarity and Naturalness}: Quantified via standardized Mean Opinion Scores (\gls{mos}) on a 5-point Likert scale, assessing how faithfully the synthesized utterance replicates the timber, pitch contour, and perceived vocal identity of the reference speaker.
    \item \textbf{Acoustic Spectral Distortion}: Measured via Mel-Cepstral Distortion (\gls{mcd}) between ground-truth and synthesized acoustic trajectories:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MCD} = \frac{10\sqrt{2}}{\ln 10} \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^D (c_t[d] - \hat{c}_t[d])^2}
    \end{equation}
    where $c_t[d]$ and $\hat{c}_t[d]$ denote the $d$-th mel-frequency cepstral coefficients of target and synthesized frames.
\end{itemize}

\subsection[Computational Throughput and RTF]{Computational Throughput and Real-Time Factor (\gls{rtf})}
Operational throughput is quantified via the \gls{rtf}, defined as the ratio between the total wall-clock computational latency required to synthesize the audio and the physical duration of the resulting synthesized speech waveform:
\begin{equation}
    \text{RTF} = \frac{T_{\text{synthesis}}}{T_{\text{audio}}} = \frac{\Delta t_{\text{proc}}}{\frac{N_{\text{samples}}}{f_s}}
    \label{eq:rtf_definition}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_{\text{proc}}$ represents execution time in seconds, $N_{\text{samples}}$ denotes synthesized audio samples, and $f_s$ represents the acoustic sampling frequency (24,000\,Hz). 

In edge environments lacking server GPUs, the primary scientific question is not whether synthesis achieves instantaneous sub-second streaming on day one, but rather whether non-autoregressive flow-matching models can execute stably within bounded, deterministic execution latencies on mobile ARM CPUs, establishing an empirical baseline for future hardware NPU acceleration.

\subsection{The Fixed Overhead Anomaly in Short Utterances}
In iterative flow-matching and diffusion systems, empirical latency characterization reveals a counter-intuitive phenomenon designated as the \textit{Fixed Overhead Anomaly}. In traditional autoregressive systems, computational latency scales quasi-linearly with output sequence length ($O(T)$). However, in non-autoregressive continuous flow matching on edge hardware, total synthesis latency decomposes into fixed initialization overhead and length-dependent numerical solving:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{synthesis}} = T_{\text{init}} + N_{\text{steps}} \cdot T_{\text{forward}}(T_{\text{audio}}) + T_{\text{vocoder}}(T_{\text{audio}})
    \label{eq:latency_decomposition}
\end{equation}
Here, $T_{\text{init}}$ encompasses tokenization, vocabulary dictionary lookup, text embedding projections, runtime memory arena configuration, and vocoder initialization. On mobile ARM hardware, $T_{\text{init}}$ constitutes an invariant baseline cost of approximately 1.0 to 1.5 seconds. 

Consequently, when synthesizing an exceptionally short sentence (e.g., a five-word utterance yielding $T_{\text{audio}} \approx 1.2$\,seconds), the invariant overhead $T_{\text{init}}$ dominates the numerator of \eref{eq:rtf_definition}, producing an anomalously high \gls{rtf} (e.g., $\text{RTF} \in [3.8, 9.3]$). As target audio duration increases to medium ($T_{\text{audio}} \approx 5.5$\,s) and long ($T_{\text{audio}} \approx 15.0$\,s) sequences, $T_{\text{init}}$ amortizes over the larger denominator, allowing the measured \gls{rtf} to drop asymptotically toward the model's true steady-state forward-pass throughput on physical ARM cores.
